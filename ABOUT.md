# MissAlign

**A browser-based optical path designer for tabletop laser labs.** Lay out a real optical
bench on screen, trace the beam through it with the physics you would use on paper, and read
off the numbers you would otherwise measure with a beam profiler — before you touch a single
mount.

Open [`simulator/simulator.html`](simulator/simulator.html) in a browser. One self-contained
file, no build step, no server, no install.

→ **[MANUAL.md](MANUAL.md)** to learn how to drive it.

---

## What it is

MissAlign sits between a sketch on a napkin and a full ray-tracing package like Zemax. It is
not a lens-design tool: it will not optimise an aspheric surface. What it does is answer the
questions that actually cost you an afternoon at the table —

- Where does the waist land if I put this lens 240 mm downstream, and how big is it?
- Will this beam still fit through the AOM aperture after the telescope?
- What coupling efficiency should I expect into that fiber, and is the beam round enough
  that a single spherical lens can fix it?
- If I fold the beam up to the MOT chamber with a periscope, what is the spot size when it
  gets there?
- Which of these two half-wave plate angles gives me the split ratio I want?

Every component maps to real hardware, and its behaviour comes from the physics — Gaussian
`q`-parameter and ABCD matrices for beam size, Jones calculus for polarisation — not from a
lookup table. World units are millimetres; every beam size is a 1/e² intensity **radius**
(quoted as `w`, with `⌀ = 2w`).

**Who it is for:** anyone building a free-space laser path on a breadboard. It was written
for an ytterbium cold-atom lab, so a few features are specific to that (a nanofiber-MOT
chamber outline, AOM double-pass helpers), but nothing about the beam physics is.

## Where it started

The first version of MissAlign was generated by a labmate, @s20000125-alt, using an LLM,
and is published at
[iams-yb-lab/frequency_shift_simulator](https://github.com/iams-yb-lab/frequency_shift_simulator).
It was already a working tool, and it established everything the current version is built
on:

- a single-file HTML app with a drag-and-drop optical bench, grid snapping and a 3D view;
- **live beam tracing** with real polarisation physics — Jones matrices through waveplates,
  PBS and mirrors, with power splitting recomputed as you rotate a plate;
- **Gaussian propagation** via the complex `q` parameter and ABCD matrices, with a `w(z)`
  caustic plot, waist markers and Rayleigh ranges;
- 19 component types (laser, mirrors, PBS/beamsplitters, waveplates, lens, AOM, EOM,
  shutter, fiber in/out, dump, power meter, …);
- the analysis tabs — target frequencies, β curves, resonator eigenmode, `w(z)` caustic,
  3D view — plus project save/load and the landing page the name comes from.

Its limits were all in the same direction: **it modelled one transverse axis, in one plane.**

| | Original | Now |
|---|---|---|
| Transverse axes tracked | 1 | 2 (astigmatic) |
| Beam leaves the board plane | no | yes (periscopes) |
| Component types | 19 | 25 |
| Top-level functions | 251 | 332 |
| Lines | 13,397 | 17,145 |

Concretely, it could not represent a cylindrical lens, a sheet beam, an elliptical
diode output, or a periscope; fiber coupling assumed you bolted an external lens in front of
the port; there was no way to produce a figure for a paper; and components could only sit on
the 25 mm hole grid, so the sub-millimetre moves that alignment actually consists of had
nowhere to live.

## What I added

Seven substantial pieces of work, then a long tail of smaller ones. Every change is recorded
per session in [`simulator/CHANGELOG.md`](simulator/CHANGELOG.md), with the narrative and the
verification evidence in [`logs/`](logs/).

### Major

#### 1. Two-axis (astigmatic) beam engine + cylindrical lens

The engine tracked a single `q`, so both transverse axes were forced to be equal. Real
tables are full of things that break that: cylindrical lenses, sheet beams, diode outputs,
tilted optics.

`beam.q` is now explicitly the **in-plane** axis, and the out-of-plane axis rides along as
`beam.qa`, an *offset*: `q_vertical = q + qa`. The offset is exactly invariant under free
propagation, which is the whole point — every `{...beam}` spread and all ~20 free-propagation
sites in the tracer stay correct untouched, and a scene with no cylindrical optics has
`qa == null` everywhere and behaves bit-for-bit as before. Only elements with optical *power*
have to remap it.

On top of that sits a **cylindrical lens** component with a selectable powered axis (in-plane
or out-of-plane), and an **astigmatic laser source** with an independent out-of-plane waist.
Two crossed cylinders of equal focal length reproduce a spherical lens on both axes, to
machine precision.

#### 2. Out-of-plane routing — V-Mirror periscopes

A top-view simulator cannot draw a beam that leaves the table, so it used to pretend that
never happens. Cold-atom labs do it constantly: you go up to the chamber and come back down.

The **V-Mirror** component folds the beam out of the board plane. Two of them on the same
channel form a periscope: the beam leaves one, runs vertically for `vpath_mm`, and re-enters
the plane at its mate with polarisation, AOM/EOM history and path length all carried across.
A single mirror can instead retro-reflect, which is how a MOT vertical arm works.

The physics that makes this more than bookkeeping: **a periscope whose exit azimuth turns 90°
exchanges the two transverse axes.** That is modelled, and it is why the astigmatic engine
above has to exist before this one can be correct.

#### 3. Fiber coupling: integrated collimators and per-axis efficiency

Fiber ports now carry an **optional built-in collimator** (`collimator_f`, `collimator_dist`),
so an A7.5-style package is one toggle instead of a hand-placed external lens you have to
remember to keep in sync. FiberOut emits the post-lens mode referenced to the port face;
FiberIn computes coupling *through* its own lens.

Coupling efficiency became astigmatism-aware. The old expression is the *square* of the
one-dimensional overlap — correct only for a round beam — so it is now
`η = η₁D(in-plane) · η₁D(out-of-plane)`, which collapses to the original expression exactly
when the beam is round. That matters because it tells you the truth an elliptical beam
hides: no single spherical lens will fix both axes, and the panel says so. The panel also
reports each axis and the ellipticity.

(The one-click auto-align solver was in the original; it was extended so its real-re-trace
scoring stays exact through the new collimator.)

#### 4. The `w(z)` beam-caustic analysis window

The caustic plot existed but showed one curve for whichever beam happened to be strongest.
It is now the main measurement instrument:

- **Pick the light, not a guess** — choose the source (laser / fiber output / periscope) and
  the branch, then trim the plotted range to any two optics on the path.
- **Both transverse axes** — a solid in-plane envelope and a dashed out-of-plane one, with
  both waists, their sizes, and the axial separation between them, which *is* the astigmatism.
- **A linked cursor.** This is the part that changes how it feels to use: probing the plot
  drops a marker on the bench canvas at the same physical plane, and probing the bench drives
  the plot's crosshair. You point at a spot on the plot and see where on the table it is.
- **Periscope runs stitched in** — the vertical leg of a periscope and the whole far side of
  the path are plotted as one continuous curve, shaded to show where the beam is off the
  board, with the height above the table read out.
- **A target-z plane** you can park anywhere to read the diameter you will actually get there,
  plus CSV export of the whole trace.

It is also **12× faster**: it used to re-sample every beam in the scene on every mouse
movement and draw one of them, which pinned the probe at 3 fps on a real layout. 243 ms →
14 ms per frame.

#### 5. Publication schematic export

One click produces a **publication-quality line-art figure** of the layout as SVG or PNG:
white background, black standard optical symbols, wavelength-coloured beams, true positions
and angles, labels that dodge the beam lines, scoped to one board or the whole scene. This
replaces redrawing the layout by hand in Illustrator every time it changes.

#### 6. Sub-hole fine alignment

Alignment is not a 25 mm grid operation. Every relevant component gained **fine-nudge
controls** — along the beam axis, perpendicular to it, and in angle — down to 0.01 mm and
0.0001°, plus typed absolute distances, a "z on beam" readout so you can position by path
length instead of coordinates, and a centre-to-centre separation control for pairs. Mirrors,
lenses, collimators and coupling lenses each get the axes that make sense for them.

This is what makes the tool answer "how far do I have to move this, and which way" rather
than just "roughly here".

#### 7. Nanofiber-MOT chamber board

The MOT chamber is now a first-class board preset, its outline, keep-out radius and ⌀60
through-hole traced from the actual assembly STL
(`assets/breadboard/`, kept out of git — see [`LOCAL-FILES.md`](LOCAL-FILES.md)). Beams interact with the chamber body and the
dispenser flat, so a path that would physically hit the chamber is blocked on screen instead
of silently passing through it.

### Minor

Smaller additions and fixes, in brief:

- **Two more components** — a **camera** (sensor size, pixel pitch, per-axis spot size in
  pixels, per-axis fill and clipping warnings) and an **iris** that passes only chosen AOM
  diffraction orders.
- **Layout ergonomics** — persistent component groups (click selects the group, Alt+click a
  member) with rigid drag, rotation and mirroring; best-fit re-landing on the hole grid after
  rotation; per-board lock/rotate; board presets; a two-component separation ruler;
  hover-only labels so the canvas stays readable while functional state stays visible.
- **Fiber plumbing** — A–H channel pairing with live pair status, NA ↔ mode-field-radius
  controls on both ends, and fiber *chains* (laser → port A … port A → optics → port B) that
  light up correctly regardless of component order.
- **Physics fixes** — removed three hidden 5 µm laser-waist clamps (the floor is now the
  diffraction limit λ/2); interaction apertures decoupled from drawn housing sizes
  (fiber ports and AOMs were grabbing beams up to 32 mm off-axis); adaptive caustic sampling
  so a µm-scale waist no longer bottoms out at 5–10 µm on the plot.
- **Caustic correctness** — the waist marker, Rayleigh band, target readout and probe were
  interpolating absolute path length against a plot-relative axis, so every one of them sat
  at the wrong `z` for any beam born at a fiber output or periscope, or any trimmed range.
- **Interface** — light/dark themes for reports, a `µm`-label rendering fix (CSS uppercase
  was turning `µ` into a Greek capital Mu, so every "(µm)" read "(MM)"), and the OS cursor
  restored while probing.
- **Dispersive optics** (added 2026-09-16 by Shayne Bennetts) — a **prism** traced through its real
  triangle at Sellmeier n(λ) (Thorlabs PS850/PS852/PS853 presets; minimum-deviation button) and a
  reflective **grating** with one traced beam per order from d(sin α + sin β) = mλ (Thorlabs GH13-24U
  preset; order chips, working-order efficiency, Littrow button). Both apply the in-plane anamorphic
  magnification to `q` and keep the reduced q̂ = q/n inside glass, so the two-axis engine stays exact —
  including the tilted-face astigmatism a prism gives a focused beam.
- **Supercontinuum source mode** (2026-09-17, Shayne Bennetts) — a laser can emit a band λ_min…λ_max on a
  nominal spacing with a spectral power density per nm, one traced beam per sample, so a prism or grating
  shows the spectrum, a dichroic splits the band and a fibre carries all of it.

## How this was built, and how it is checked

Both the original and my additions were written with LLM assistance — this repo does not
pretend otherwise, and [`logs/`](logs/) contains the full session-by-session record,
including the design decisions that turned out to be wrong and had to be reversed.

What makes that trustworthy is the verification, which is documented in
[`simulator/HANDOVER.md`](simulator/HANDOVER.md) and applied to every change:

1. **Parse** every inline script (there is no build step to catch a syntax error for you).
2. **Assert the physics against closed-form Gaussians** computed independently — per-axis
   `w(z)`, crossed cylinders reproducing a spherical lens, coupling η reducing exactly to the
   old expression for a round beam.
3. **Drive the real app** in headless Chrome over the DevTools protocol and check end-to-end
   behaviour, including every panel and every selectable beam on a real layout.
4. **Fingerprint a regression**: hash every segment endpoint, every `q`, every coupling
   efficiency for a real 265-beam scene before and after a change, and require them to match
   unless the change was meant to alter them.

That last one is why the astigmatism work can be stated as a fact rather than a hope: the
real lab layout (265 beams, 1903 segments) traces to a **bit-identical** fingerprint before
and after, so existing scenes are provably unaffected.
